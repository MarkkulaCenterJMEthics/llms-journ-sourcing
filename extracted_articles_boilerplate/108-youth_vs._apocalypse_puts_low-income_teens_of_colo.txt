Title: Youth vs. Apocalypse Puts Low-Income Teens of Color First
Author: Brooke Anderson
Date: 2023-04-11
URL: https://www.teenvogue.com/story/youth-vs-apocalypse
==================================================

This piece was written as part of a reporting fellowship from Movement Generation Justice & Ecology Project.
Lizbeth Ibarra, 18, grew up in the shadow of the Chevron refinery in Richmond, California. She was eight years old when the refinery exploded, sending 15,000 people to area hospitals with breathing troubles. Later, her grandmother, who worked nights as a janitor at the refinery, got cancer. Her dad developed asthma, as did many of Ibarra’s childhood friends.
“Still, growing up, we’d talk about Chevron in a good way. They give scholarships, sponsor the newspaper, and employ hella people,” Ibarra recalls. It wasn’t until Youth vs. Apocalypse (YVA) came to her class that it clicked for her: “They’re poisoning our lungs, the air, the soil. Refineries are often placed in communities, like Richmond, with low-income people of color. You don’t see that where there’s mostly white people. It’s environmental racism.”
YVA is a youth-led, Bay Area-based collective of young climate justice activists. Ibarra says she and many of her peers were compelled to join the climate justice movement because of YVA’s ability to connect the lived-daily experiences of pollution, poverty, and racism to accelerating climate chaos.
“You can’t talk about the [climate] problem as though it’s about polar bears and melting ice caps. You have to talk about racism, capitalism, sexism," says Ibarra. “It’s putting profit over people and treating people like we’re disposable. Everything is connected.”
Youth vs. Apocalypse’s approach has been strikingly effective. In 2016, the group was instrumental in winning a citywide ban (now being contested by the developer) on coal storage and transport. In 2019, YVA rose to national fame when it confronted California senator Diane Feinstein about her refusal to support the Green New Deal; in a video that went viral, Sen. Feinstein dismissed the group's concerns by saying, “How old are you?... You didn’t vote for me.” And that fall, YVA helped drive more than 30,000 people to turn out to the youth climate strike in San Francisco. Currently, the organization is fighting to divest teachers’ pensions from fossil fuel companies, to end oil and gas extraction, and to pass the Green New Deal.
I ask YVA leaders what lessons they would share with teens across the country who want to organize for climate justice in their own schools. One suggestion: Use this upcoming Earth Day, April 22, to plan activities at your school. YVA suggests young people offer to do presentations in classes with supportive teachers, create social media content, conduct a postering campaign, paint a mural, host a speak-out, or get a school or district to support a resolution demanding climate action.
“The climate justice movement has historically been portrayed as a white-led movement, focusing on the use of plastic straws while ignoring the intentional poisoning of Black and brown communities,” YVA organizer Aniya Butler, 17, says. “With Earth Day actions, youth of color can show the power we have and use it to fight for a sustainable and equitable future. This is a movement for us by us.”
Read on for additional advice.
Frontline youth: Center the leadership of the most impacted youth.
Youth vs. Apocalypse is led by and centers frontline youth like Ibarra, young people who are Black, Indigenous, immigrants, and people of color, and especially youth from poor and working-class families who are most impacted by the climate crisis and its root causes.
“We as young people of color coming from low-income communities have not all seen ourselves in this movement,” says YVA’s education and organizing coordinator, Hannah Estrada, 20. “But we’re going to be the most impacted, so it’s important that we are involved. In the climate movement, there is a place for us.”
She adds, "What we want as young people of color is for other young people of color to see us — to highlight frontline communities."
For YVA, prioritizing frontline youth isn’t just about who is on the mic or in front of the cameras. YVA members choose campaigns, strategies, and tactics based on their lived experiences. This brings youth into the organization who don’t consider themselves activists or “into politics”; they may not feel comfortable using a bullhorn, but they want to write poetry, make a TikTok, or paint a mural.
The climate crisis is just a symptom: Uproot the entire system.
According to YVA, centering frontline youth means meeting fellow students where they are on the issues that impact them every day. Says Estrada, “We don’t see the climate crisis as separate from colonialism or white supremacy, but a symptom of the same system. The climate crisis intersects other issues — food deserts, poverty, violence. We can’t pretend we’re not fighting these other traumas."
She continues, "You can’t expect frontline communities to pick one issue. When we’re asking people to come into the movement, we’re not asking them to change the way they are, but to bring their whole selves and skills.”
YVA’s Points of Unity reflects this approach and includes positions against white supremacy, for the Black Lives Matter movement, abolishing the police, ICE, and the prison industrial complex, supporting Indigenous sovereignty, challenging rape culture, and providing housing, health care, and meaningful work for all.
Squad up on campus: Find supportive teachers, speak in classes, and build clubs.
YVA uses a school-based community organizing model to build its foundation. Students ask their teachers to allow YVA members to speak in their classes, using the group's downloadable curricula. Afterward, YVA signs up students for weekly club meetings at their school — with free pizza, thanks to the generosity of a local worker cooperative, Arizmendi Pizza.
At club meetings, YVA plugs students into the larger climate space and shares information about upcoming mobilizations. The group maps out relationships at school, identifying supportive teachers. From those clubs, YVA selects young people, ages 11-20, to deepen its leadership base through paid fellowships.
Cory Jong teaches eighth-grade ethnic studies at Urban Promise Academy in Oakland, CA. She cared about energy efficiency and recycling in college, she recalls, but “the environmental clubs were so white. It didn’t feel like a space for me as someone who has experienced racism, as a survivor of sexual assault. I couldn’t support organizing where I had to cut myself in pieces.”
As a teacher, Jong supports her students in building YVA to be the kind of safe, intersectional political home that she needed when she was a young activist. Given that many of her students are dealing with the presence of gangs, gun violence, and incarceration, Jong is developing a climate justice curriculum that incorporates those realities — and asking other teachers to join her in the process.
“I don’t see myself as an adult ally," says Jong. "I really look at it as a co-conspirator.”
The more you know: Develop and share climate curricula.
Youth vs. Apocalypse has developed a downloadable climate justice curriculum tool kit that connects what students are experiencing in their own lives to the unfolding ecological crisis. “If you ask, ‘Are you impacted by climate change?,’ no one raises their hand. But if you ask, ‘Have you breathed wildfire smoke?,' everyone raises their hand,” says Carolyn Norr, a former teacher who cofounded YVA and serves as its program director.
Those in-person conversations are key for YVA members like Estrada. “It’s different than just posting on Instagram,” she says. “When you’re in the classroom and able to talk face-to-face, they understand how [the climate movement] can be a place for them.”
If it’s not soulful, it’s not strategic: Deploy arts and culture.
YVA uses art, spoken word, hip-hop, murals, and social media to make the movement appeal to young people and to build popular support for its campaigns. Aniya is YVA’s hip-hop and climate justice initiative coordinator. She came to the organization in 2019, after YVA held a meeting at the school where her mom worked. Aniya is a spoken-word poet, so YVA asked her to perform at an upcoming climate strike.
“I haven’t always had the skills of an activist. I used poetry to explain the need for justice,” says Aniya. “Not everyone wants to write a speech, lead a strike, or write a media advisory. But everyone has a creative side. At actions, we’ll play E-40 or Too $hort. It makes the work more healing and joyful. The things we’re fighting against are draining. Music connects us.”
Aniya says that collaborative music videos like YVA’s “No One Is Disposable” track, which dropped on Earth Day 2020, kept teens engaged during the pandemic after schools shifted to remote-learning protocols.
We’ve got us: Take care of yourself, and each other.
Amid the rising climate anxiety that young people experience, Youth vs. Apocalypse prioritizes conversations about mental health and making activism sustainable. “When you’re building a movement, you have to replenish each other and yourself. You have to be in communities and develop relationships with people. It’s not transactional,” says Estrada. “We have to take care of each other. It isn’t just about protests. Sometimes it’s picking up young people, sending food to families we know are struggling, helping pay someone’s light bills.”
Aniya adds, “Take breaks. You don’t have to commit every part of your life to do this. Do things that typical teenagers do. We’re making sure we’re not sacrificing our whole childhood to this.”
As YVA builds youth power in the Bay Area, students elsewhere are replicating and adapting its model. Sim Bilal, 20, an organizer with Youth Climate Strike in Los Angeles, says, “We’ve been working with YVA since 2019. We go up to the Bay. They come down to LA. We work on actions together. I’m in awe of them — their structure and diverse membership. We’re adopting their school club model.”
As wildfires rage and seas rise, more youth are feeling the words of Sonali Mascarenhas-Swan, now 15, who said at the 2019 YVA-led youth climate strike: “Adults are f***ing up my future, and I’m done with it.”
YVA’s frontline leadership, intersectionality, downloadable curriculum, school clubs, use of art and music, and its culture of collective care are giving youth a blueprint for how to fight back — and win — in their communities.
