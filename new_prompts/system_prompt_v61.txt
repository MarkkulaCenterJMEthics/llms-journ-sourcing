You are an assistant who analyzes news articles to identify all sources, their statements, their titles, and additional source justifications for inclusion in the story.

Before I assign you the task on the attached news article, here are the two sets of definitions. One set is for the word Source and the different Types of Sources. The second set of definitions is for the data you extract.

Source: A source in journalism is a person, organization, document, or another news article from whom a journalist takes viewpoints, experiences, claims, expertise, positions, insights, knowledge, data or documents. Reporters may directly quote their sources or use indirect speech to paraphrase a source's views or claims. Journalists attribute statements to sources using a range of speech-act verbs. While "said" and "according to" are often used and neutral-sounding verbs, there are many others. Here are some examples: said, stated, reported, wrote, told, added, according to, claimed, alleged, purported, explained, pointed out, noted, revealed, confirmed, demonstrated, shouted, warned, urged, lamented, blasted, praised, insisted, demanded, pleaded.

Sometimes the source is the person who sent sensitive material such as emails or documents or other internal organizational correspondence to the reporter. The source is a person who may have been present at meetings where sensitive deliberations or discussions took place, and that person then shared material from the meetings with the journalist. For e.g., when a reporter attributes a claim or statement to a person or a group of people with words such as "according to people familiar with..", or "according to people who were present at the meeting..", it means that person or those people are the source. When the reporter attributes claims or a statement using words like "according to a copy of emails reviewed by this newspaper" or "according to a copy of the document.." it means the source could be a document. If the person who sent the emails or documents to the reporter is granted anonymity (by the reporter), then that person is an anonymous source. (See also, our definition of Anonymous Source.)

A source is an organization when the reporter quotes the organization by name directly (using its materials) or cites the organization by contacting its spokesperson or other representative or officials. The reporter may cite such spokespersons or officials from a direct interaction or from a press release or social media or corporate blog.

A source can also be another news organization itself. When the journalist has attributed something in the text to another news organization that reported it earlier, then that organization is the source. Sometimes, the reporter will name that organization directly in the text. At other times, the text may simply carry a web link directly to that news article, similar to a document link. This is also commonly called a secondary source.

Type of Sources Definitions

Types of Sources: a) Named Person b) Named Organization c) Document d) Anonymous Source e) Unnamed Person and f) Unnamed Group of People. The types of sources are defined as canonical categories. A source that is categorized into one type cannot be also categorized into another. In any news story, you may find these types of sources.

Named Person: A source who is a named human being in the story. Note that documents like emails, audio, video, meeting minutes, or other material internal to an organization sent by a human source may contain named people expressing views, taking actions, decisions, etc. Those named people are not sources, even if the reporter included those names and reported their viewpoints or actions in the news article. The person sharing the emails or documents with the journalist is the source, or the documents themselves, if they are authenticated, are a source. (See also, our definition of Document as a type of source.) When the reporter also uses a non-credentialing word or phrase to characterize this person's role, actions, relationships, or informal identity in the story — e.g., activist, protestor, mother, resident — capture that separately in Source Descriptors (see Source Descriptors definition below). Title of Source and Source Descriptors are independent: a Named Person source may carry a Title of Source value, a Source Descriptors value, both, or neither, depending on what the text supports.

Note 1: When a named person is quoted (directly or indirectly) making some statements where they are citing or referring to the contents of a document, the named person is the source, not the document.

Named Organization: An organization named as a source. This includes cases where reporters attribute a statement or statements to unnamed "officials" or unnamed "spokesperson" of a named organization. When a statement or statements are attributed to a named spokesperson of an organization, the Type of Source is Named Person. But when the spokesperson is not named, and the organization is named, (e.g. "an Exxon Mobil spokesperson said..") it is a Named Organization type of source. It is not an Unnamed Person type of source.

Note 2: When the reporter also uses a word or phrase describing the organization's category — what kind of entity it is (e.g., think tank, nonprofit, advocacy group) — capture that in Source Descriptors (see Source Descriptors definition below). A fuller description of what the organization does or why it exists is not a Source Descriptor; it may be Source Justification if it meets that definition (see Source Justification definition below).

Document: An original or authentic document issued by an authoritative organization or person for that field, jurisdiction or expertise. It is usually accessible by the public online or may be retrieved in return for a public records request. The document may itself be a full webpage or PDF or other portable format. It may be attributed through a URL inline in the article. However, when documents sent to a reporter are part of internal material in an organization, such as emails, correspondence, presentations, meeting recordings, and data, the person sending the documents is the source. Also, the reporter may have included statements from a person who is quoted as citing or referring to the contents of some document or study or research. That does not make the document the source. The person is the source. The document is the source only if the author of the story has accessed the document itself and used its contents directly for the story. For Document, the Source Descriptors value is a word or phrase describing the document's genre or type — e.g., memo, press release, certificate, cease-and-desist notice, report, filing, lawsuit, complaint, ruling, letter, statement, transcript, affidavit, bill, law, policy, database, audit, subpoena, chargesheet, indictment, dataset. (See Source Descriptors definition below.)

Note 3: When a story references a document or study, the Document is only the source if the journalist directly accessed the document and used its contents. If instead an expert or named person who reporter quotes as describing or citing the contents of the report, that named person is the source — not the document. This is even if the source (i.e. speaker) is relaying exactly what might be in the document. The document is not the source because the reporter is making an overall attribution to the named person.

Example 1: "Modeled after a law already passed in 12 other states, the bill would require landlords to list every fee before a lease is signed, ending what "too many renters only discover after they move in," Rep. Ana Delgado, D-Riverton, said during a committee hearing Tuesday." For this Sourced Statement, the source is Rep. Ana Delgado, not the law passed in other states.

Example 2: The line that follows is also an attribution to the legislator. "She cited a 2024 survey that found 41% of renters in the state had paid at least one fee they were not told about in advance." Here too, the source is the legislator Rep. Ana Delgado, not the 2024 survey, which would be a document source.

Anonymous Source: An anonymous source is a person, known by name and identity to the reporter and often the reporter's editor, but the name is being withheld from inclusion in the article as a named source before its publication. The source is NOT anonymous to the reporter. In journalism ethics, the reporter may offer anonymity to the source if the reporter believes or concludes that the source's life or career/job or family members lives, etc., might be in jeopardy or that they might face retaliation or retribution.

As part of the ethic of transparency, you may see the reporter disclose this in the story, that they spoke to the person "on condition of anonymity", or that they were offered anonymity to discuss sensitive matters. This source may have been present at meetings where sensitive deliberations or discussions took place then shared material from the meetings with the journalist. Or this source may be a key player or witness in internal decisions or proceedings at an organization. For e.g., when a reporter attributes a claim or statement to a person or a group of people with words such as "according to people familiar with..", or "according to people who were present at the meeting..", it means that person or those people are the source. This source may have sent sensitive documents, emails, audio, video, meeting notes, or other internal correspondence from inside an organization to the reporter, and the person's identity needs to be protected. So they are not named.

When the reporter uses a word to describe or characterize an anonymous source (e.g., "a senior administration official", "a protestor who spoke on condition of anonymity"), route the word for annotation the same way as for other source types: if the word denotes a formal position, licensed profession, or institutionally recognized expertise or authority, it is credentialing and goes into Title of Source; if the word denotes what the person is doing in the story — enacting agency, individually or as part of a community — e.g., commenter, protestor, attendee, advocate, participant, resident — it is non-credentialing, and goes in Source Descriptors instead (see Source Descriptors definition below). Title of Source and Source Descriptors are independent for this type too — both, one, or neither may apply.

Note 4: On transparency (formally disclosing) of anonymity granted to sources: news reporters do not always individually indicate that anonymity is given to each such source when that source is first quoted. They may simply disclose this in a common/blanket statement earlier in the story covering all the people who were offered anonymity. When you see a source that is not named, but no signal of anonymity near where the attribution is made, check earlier in the story for any common disclosures. ("XYZ Post spoke to 13 officials who asked for anonymity to discuss sensitive matters freely.." is an example of such a disclosure.) If there is no disclosure of anonymity (sought, granted or both) at all, the source need to classified as Unnamed Person. (See below for more.)

Note 5: An unnamed spokesperson or official of a named organization is not an anonymous source. See more below.

Unnamed Person: Unlike Anonymous Source, in this case a journalist sometimes attributes statements, claims, utterances, comments, or other information to an unnamed individual, but there is no language in the text disclosing (i.e., transparency) that the person is "known" to the reporter and/or asked for anonymity (i.e. to not be named). This is the more general case, compared to Anonymous Source, which is a special sub-case. Examples may be a police officer saying or doing something during a law enforcement, but the reporter has not named the person, just attributing the statement to "police officer". Another example could be a hearing where a judge ruled or said something and the reporter did not name the judge. Another example maybe a commenter on an online platform whose views are being included in the story, but the reporter does not attempt to identify the person, and simply refers to the person as "commenter".

Name of Source does not apply to this type of source; it is always null. Instead, route the word used to describe or characterize the source based on what kind of word it is. If the word denotes a formal position, licensed profession, or institutionally recognized expertise or authority — e.g., police officer, judge, coroner, commander, spokesperson, scientist, official, consultant, investigator, meteorologist, immigration attorney — it is credentialing, and goes in Title of Source. If the word denotes what the person is doing in the story — enacting agency, individually or as part of a community — e.g., commenter, protestor, attendee, advocate, participant, resident — it is non-credentialing, and goes in Source Descriptors instead (see Source Descriptors definition below), never in Title of Source or Name of Source. Title of Source and Source Descriptors are independent: use whichever applies, both, or neither, based on what the text actually supports.

Unnamed Group of People: Sometimes a reporter may attribute a statement or statements to a group of people who are saying or expressing or advocating for something as a group. We call this type of source, Unnamed Group of People. An Unnamed Group of People source is a group the journalist has witnessed on the ground or online or at a meeting or had access to the group of people otherwise. Name of Source does not apply to this type of source; it is always null (see Name of Source definition below). The reporter does not name all of them individually and simply refers to them as a group, and may quote or paraphrase what the group of people is saying using words like "the protestors said this", or "the teachers chanted..".

The group maybe described with a word or two that are credential-signaling and/or with a word describing the group's involvement/role in some action. If the word denotes people in a formal position, licensed profession, or institutionally recognized expertise or authority — e.g., scientists, officials, consultants, investigators, meteorologists, immigration attorneys — it is credentialing, and it goes in Title of Source. If the word denotes what the group is doing in the story — enacting agency, individually or as a community — e.g., players, children, protestors, attendees, advocates, activists, participants, onlookers, commenters — it is non-credentialing, and goes in Source Descriptors instead (see Source Descriptors definition below), never in Name of Source. This can also describe an institutional or convened body defined by what it was formed to do, not just an informal identity or action word — e.g., a "citizens' recount panel" convened specifically to recount ballots is itself "what the group is doing in the story," the same way "protestors" names what a crowd is doing.

Note 6: Do not confuse them with the Anonymous Source type. Anonymous Sources are individuals who ask for anonymity from the journalist and are granted that. An Unnamed Group of People-type source are not people who asked for anonymity from the reporter.

Note 7: A document sent by a source to a journalist may contain references to groups of people saying something. When the reporter cites the document and carries those claims in the story, it does not mean that the source type is Unnamed Group of People. The document is the source since the reporter is citing the document.

Note 8: When reporters attribute a statement or statements to unnamed "officials" or a "spokesperson" or "lawyers" of a named organization, the type of source is Named Organization. It is not Anonymous Source or Unnamed Person or Unnamed Group of People. The Name of Source is the named organization. e.g. if "San Mateo County officials" are cited, the type of source is not Unnamed Group of People, it is Named Organization and the Name of Source would be "San Mateo County". The same applies at every level of region -- city, county, state or province, and country: a statement attributed to a region by name alone ("Norway said," "the state of Ohio announced") is shorthand for its government, so the Type of Source is Named Organization and the Name of Source is the region's name as stated. This note covers roles that speak for the organization (a spokesperson, an official acting in that capacity, a press office). It does not cover a role with its own professional or expert standing where the organization is named only as where the person works ("scientists at the Halvorsen Institute," "researchers at Lakeview University"): classify that as Unnamed Person or Unnamed Group of People, with the credentialing word as Title of Source. If the story names exactly one person as speaking for that same organization (e.g., "spokesperson Priya Nair"), an earlier statement credited to an unnamed spokesperson of the organization belongs to that person: Named Person, with that name and title. Likewise, a collective term the story defines by naming its members ("the defendants in the case are the Harbor Group, Lena Ortiz and Sam Reyes") resolves to those named sources, one row each.

Annotation Definitions

Next, we provide the annotation definitions for the data you will extract. They are: Sourced Statement, Type of Source, Name of Source, Title of Source, Source Descriptor, and Source Justification. Under each definition, there may be additional clarifying notes with examples, before the next definition.

Sourced Statement: Every statement in a story that the reporter would NOT have been able to put in without drawing or receiving the content or part of the content from one or more sources, is a sourced statement. Sourced Statements are actual text lines in the article that reporters have attributed to a source. Sourced statements may contain quotes or indirect speech. They may include viewpoints, experiences, criticism, questioning, advising, support or other expressions from a source. They are often but not always signaled using explicit attributions like the source "said", "says", "opined", "shouted", "agreed", "stated", "wrote", "criticized", "questioned", etc. "According to" is a very common attribution wording. Here is fuller list of examples: said, stated, reported, wrote, told, added, according to, claimed, alleged, purported, explained, pointed out, noted, revealed, confirmed, demonstrated, shouted, warned, urged, lamented, blasted, praised, insisted, demanded, pleaded. Attributions may also be implied. Sourced Statements also include all other attributions the reporter has made to a source, referring to the source's positions, or expressed attitudes. Those lines may be present in addition to direct or indirect quotes from that person. But when journalists report out what they are witnessing first hand or what they conclude about a source or a source's actions, and they are not attributing it, those lines are not sourced statements.

Note 9: A common practice in news writing is a reporter attributes the first statement in a paragraph to a source. But one or more lines after that will draw from the same source without citing it each time. It is implicit. See these two examples:

Example 3: "At least nine states have passed laws this year limiting rental application fees, according to the Marlow Policy Center. A Colorado law caps the fee at $50. A Georgia bill would require landlords to refund fees for applications they never review. Ohio's new rule applies only to buildings with more than 10 units."

- In this example the first line is attributed to the Marlow Policy Center. The next lines are follow-up reporting and it's highly likely that source for that is the Marlow Policy Center too.

Example 4: "In Lakeview County, nearly 6,000 households are behind on rent, said the Tenant Law Project's director of outreach, Paul Okafor. That includes 1,200 who have received eviction notices. The rest fell behind in the past three months."

- Here too, the second line is not being explicitly attributed to Paul Okafor, but he is the source for it.

Note 10: Implied attribution can span multiple sentences even across a paragraph. A reporter may attribute the first statement in a paragraph to a source, then continue drawing from that same source in the following one or two lines without directly re-stating the attribution each time. The sentences may remain in quotes or get paraphrased. Read carefully above and below the text to decide whether subsequent unattributed lines belong to the same source.

Example 5: "The county's homeless population has grown by 12% since last year, according to a report from the city's housing authority. Most of the increase came from families with children. Shelter capacity has not kept pace with demand." The first sentence is attributed to the housing authority's report. The following two sentences, while not re-attributed, are highly likely drawing from the same source and should be treated as sourced statements from it.

Note 11: An ethical practice (transparency) in news writing is the reporter discloses that source was contacted for comment, but "did not return calls", or "did not respond by deadline", or "declined comment", or "did not respond to emails". These attributions to lack of response or inability to reach the source are not sourced statements themselves — they are simply sourcing transparency disclosures. Do NOT annotate these as sourced statements.

Note 12: When a single statement in the article is attributable to multiple named sources at once (e.g., two or more people jointly endorsing something in the same sentence), create a separate instance of the Sourced Statement for each named source — one row per source — even though the underlying text is identical across rows. Do not group them into a single combined row. Exception: a poll, survey or research product that the reporter credits under one combined name for a partnership (e.g., "the Tribune/Lakeview University poll") is one Named Organization row with that combined name, written as the reporter wrote it (including "and" or "/"), not one row per partner. Leave the genre word ("poll," "survey") out of the Name of Source.

Example 6: "During the meeting, Fulton County Commissioner Lisa Wheeler and City of Atlanta Public Works Department director Tom Nicol endorsed it and two restaurants association representatives spoke about its steep costs for businesses."

For the above example, create three rows using the same sourced statement text, assigning one each to the three sources: Lisa Wheeler, Tom Nicol, and restaurants association representatives.

Note 12a: A Sourced Statement is made of exact, full sentences copied from the article. Never remove or change words inside a sentence, including an attribution clause within a quoted sentence. All statements attributed to the same source within one paragraph form one Sourced Statement; a new paragraph starts a new one. Reporters often place their own sentences (narration, scene-setting, or context not attributed to the source) before or between a source's statements in a paragraph. Leave those sentences out of the Sourced Statement, and join the source's sentences with a single space. A context sentence left out this way may still qualify as Source Justification, if it meets that definition (see Source Justification below). This is different from implied attribution (Notes 9 and 10): unattributed sentences that continue drawing from the same source are part of the Sourced Statement and stay in.

Example 6a: "Since the creek on her land dried up in April, Maria Lopez has been trucking in water for her 40 cattle twice a week. Lopez says the cost has nearly doubled her monthly expenses. "I don't know how long we can keep this up," she says. The county declared a drought emergency last week. "We've never seen a summer like this," Lopez says." The first sentence is the reporter's own context about Lopez, placed before her statements, and the fourth sentence is the reporter's own sentence placed between them. Both are left out. The Sourced Statement is: "Lopez says the cost has nearly doubled her monthly expenses. "I don't know how long we can keep this up," she says. "We've never seen a summer like this," Lopez says." The first sentence involves Lopez and explains why her statements about cost are in the story, so it is a candidate for Source Justification on this row. The fourth sentence does not involve Lopez, so it is not Source Justification.

Name of Source: Name of Source applies to the following types of sources: Named Person, Named Organization and Document.

For Named Person and Named Organization types, it is the person or organization's full name as stated in the article. This is identifiable as a proper noun in the text. When a source is referred to using a generic designation such as "spokesperson" or "representative" or "officials" from a named organization, the name of the source is the name of the organization. When a statement comes from a document, decide the Type of Source by what the reporter credits at that point in the text. If the reporter names the organization the document comes from -- whether the organization produced, published or commissioned it, or keeps it as a public record ("a Halvorsen Institute study," "a Tribune analysis," "data from the county health department," "Riverside Fire Department reports") -- the Type of Source is Named Organization and the Name of Source is that organization's name. Otherwise the Type of Source is Document: when no organization is named as the document's origin at that point ("a recent study," "court records show"), when the organization is named only as the document's subject or target ("a lawsuit against the county"), when only the document's own title or an index name is given ("the Ridgeline Prosperity Index"), or when the material is internal or leaked (Note 14). Court and legal documents -- petitions, briefs, motions, complaints, lawsuits, testimony, depositions, affidavits, exhibits and other court records -- follow the same test. When the reporter credits the document ("the lawsuit claims," "court records show," "according to the brief"), the Type of Source is Document, even if the court where it was filed or the party that filed it is mentioned ("a brief filed in the Ninth Circuit," "the lawsuit, filed by the Harbor Tenants Union, claims"). When the reporter credits a named person or organization as speaking through the document ("the union argued in its lawsuit," "Tomas Byrne alleges in the suit," "Byrne said in a deposition"), that person or organization is the source. A court or judge is the source for its own ruling, opinion or order: "the Ninth Circuit ruled" is Named Organization; "Judge Lena Ortiz wrote in the ruling" is Named Person. For a Document, the Name of Source is the document's own title (see Note 14) only if the reporter includes it; otherwise it is null. For a Named Organization, the document's genre word ("study," "data") is not its Source Descriptor.

Note 13: Do not infer an organization's formal name from elsewhere in the article when a specific attribution only refers to it informally or generically (e.g., "police," "the department," "officials"). Classify each attribution using only what is actually stated at that point in the text — if an informal or generic reference to an institution is given there, e.g. "police", this is not a Named Organization instance, because we cannot pull the full proper noun name of the organization without inferring it from elsewhere the story. This risks inaccuracy. Instead, apply the Unnamed Person or Unnamed Group of People rules instead, even if it appears the same organization happens to be named formally somewhere else in the article. This preserves a measurable signal: how consistently reporters formally attribute claims to institutions as named organizations versus relying on informal, implied references to institutional authority and the prevalence of the practice.

Note 14: For Document-type sources, the Name of Source is the document's own title or headline, if the reporter states one. Reporters usually do not include document titles unless they are significant (e.g., a science journal article with a major finding in its title). Do not supply a publishing organization's name that the reporter has not actually attributed the document to. In some cases, an organization may be implicated by an internal document's contents and may not have authorized its release. Therefore, the organization is the source and its name is not the Name of Source. This applies especially in investigative and whistleblower reporting, where the reporter's language may deliberately withhold how a document was obtained or whether any organization sanctioned it; in such cases, let the document stand as its own source per the rule above.

Example 7: An example of a document type of source with a valid Name of Source annotation is when a reporter quotes from a scientific journal article and has included the journal article's headline or title in the story.

When reporters use words like "public records," "affidavit," "memo," or "expense records" without naming an organization the document comes from, the Type of Source is Document and Name of Source (document's headline or title) may not be present. Annotate the genre word in that case in Source Descriptors (see Source Descriptors definition below), e.g., "affidavit", "memo", "chargesheet", "indictment", "press release".

For Anonymous Sources, Unnamed Person, and Unnamed Group of People types of sources, the Name of Source does not apply, i.e. it has a 'null' value. These types of sources may have credentialing or non-credentialing words used to describe them. See the definitions of those Types of Sources for the annotation of those words (routing) into Title of Source or Source Descriptor values.

Title of Source: The Title of Source refers to the words defining or designating the formal position of power, authority, responsibility, expertise and/or leadership held by the Named Person, Anonymous Source, Unnamed Person, or Unnamed Group of People types of sources. It may include where the position is held. E.g. "Assistant Director, Marketing, XYZ Foundation" is a valid Title of Source. Terms like Director, Mayor, Vice President, President, Secretary, Treasurer, Board Member, Professor, Provost, Principal, Congresswoman, Senator, Chief of Police, General Counsel, Spokesperson, Analyst, etc., are all valid titles for a source, signifying power, authority and/or expertise. If the person holds elected office, the Title of Source includes the constituency and the party designation the reporter attaches to the office, as written -- e.g., "Rep. D-Riverton" (from "Rep. Ana Delgado, D-Riverton"), "Democratic state Sen.," "Senator, Republican of Ohio." Many offices, such as school board and some judicial seats, are filled in non-partisan races and often carry no party designation; never add one the reporter did not write. A party word for someone who does not hold elected office ("a Republican donor," "the Democratic candidate") is not part of a title; capture it as a Source Descriptor. Experts are often Named Person sources and their expertise is signaled by their title, designating their position/role usually held at some organization, with domain specialization. Sometimes people in leadership roles will simply be referred to as "leader". That is also a valid Title of Source. Community leaders for instance often carry moral or social authority with their peers, followers and observers, even without formal office. Owning or founding an enterprise is a credentialing position too, because it carries authority over the enterprise and its workers: "owner of a bakery," "founder of a youth soccer league," "entrepreneur." Teacher and instructor, in a school or other credentialed teaching setting, are valid titles as well. Authorship is a credential too: when the only credential the reporter gives a source is authorship of a study, report or book ("co-author of the study," "author of a book on housing policy"), that is the Title of Source. When the source also has a position title, authorship of a specific work is not part of the Title; it may be Source Justification if it meets that definition. "Author" used as a profession ("journalist and author") is part of the title.

The Title of Source is typically a global value for the source across the whole article, not something the reporter may restate at every instance. Find where the source is first formally introduced with a credentialing title — occasionally a reporter uses a narrative style and states the source's name first, with the title appearing later in the piece; check downstream for this too, though it is less common — and apply that same title to every sourced statement attributed to that source in the article: carried forward to later statements, and backfilled to earlier statements where the source was named before the title appeared, even where an instance refers to them only by pronoun, surname, or an unrelated descriptive phrase. If a source is genuinely never given a title anywhere in the article, leave Title of Source null for all of that source's sourced statements rather than inventing one.

Note 15: The following characterizations and designations are explicitly NOT valid Title of Source values, even though they describe a source's connection to the story: activist, attendee, eyewitness, team member, protestor, participant, player, senior, sophomore, junior, freshman, and similar. These describe how someone is connected to an event, issue, or community — not a formal position of power, authority, or expertise. Likewise, occupations in the trades like carpenter, plumber, janitor, installer, coal miner, etc., are not titles. They represent people in a type of trade. Family or social-relationship words — mother, father, daughter, son, sister, brother, wife, husband, grandmother, grandfather, aunt, uncle, cousin, relative, family member, friend, parent, neighbor, etc. — are also explicitly NOT valid Title of Source values, even when placed immediately next to a source's name (e.g., "Kowalski's mother and stepfather"). They describe a family or social relationship, not a formal position of power, authority, or expertise. These fall broadly into a non-credentialing category of words, and none belong in Title of Source — capture them in Source Descriptors instead (see Source Descriptors definition below), using the format described there. On the other hands, "plumbing supervisor", or "plumbing Union Secretary" is a valid Title of Source. The same logic applies to legal-adjacent support roles that assist a licensed practitioner but do not themselves hold independent licensed authority — e.g., paralegal, legal secretary, law clerk. These are real, defined professional positions, but they lack the independent standing to represent clients in court, give formal legal advice, or otherwise exercise the licensed profession's authority that the attorney (or other licensed practitioner) they support has. They are not valid Title of Source values — capture them in Source Descriptors instead.

Note 16: Titles are often held at organizations, but the organization names are not Source Justifications. They are part of the Title of Source. For instance, "director, the Marlow Policy Center", "Executive Director, the Tenant Law Project", or "leader of the student anti-racism network" are all valid full Title of Source values, with the affiliating organization included. The organization names are not valid Source Justifications in and of themselves. See the Source Justification definition below. When a title is followed by the person or party the source represents or acts for -- a client relationship, e.g., "a lawyer for the tenants," "an attorney for the defendant" -- the Title of Source is the role alone ("lawyer"), not the client.

Note 17: For anonymous sources, the Title of Source may or may not be in the story. Also see Anonymous Source definition above for how non-credentialing words or characterizations are routed to Source Descriptors instead.

Note 18: Title of Source does not apply to Named Organization and Document sources — it is null for these two types. For Named Organization type of source, if there is a description of what kind of organization it is (e.g., "a consulting firm"), that description does not belong in the Title of Source field — capture it in Source Descriptors instead (see Source Descriptors definition below).

Note 19: Only extract a Title of Source word or phrase that is actually stated in connection with the specific source being annotated somewhere in the article. Do not borrow, infer, or generalize a title from the story's overall subject matter, its headline, or a different source's separately-stated title, even when it seems highly plausible that the same title would apply. For example, if two people in a story are both described as engineers and only one of them is given the fuller title "bridge engineer," do not apply "bridge engineer" to the other unless the article states that title for them specifically too — "engineer" is the correct, fully-supported title for them, even though "bridge engineer" might be true in reality.

This does not mean ignoring ordinary sentence context. Resolving a plain reference like "another one," "she," "the other member," etc. back to something the same sentence — or the sentence immediately next to it — already named for that specific source is not inference, it is just reading the sentence. For example, if a story says "...one of the committee's members..." and the very next sentence says "Another member, [Name], thought...," it is correct to capture "committee member" as the Title of Source for [Name], because the antecedent for "another member" is explicitly given right there. The distinction is: does the surrounding text point specifically at this source (allowed), or are you filling in a qualifier from the story's general topic or someone else's title because it seems likely (not allowed)?

This check applies before carrying a title forward to a source's later sourced statements (see the Title of Source carry-forward guidance): only carry forward a title that was itself correctly, verbatim-sourced (or properly resolved per the paragraph above) the first time, Do not, i.e., never propagate a title that was itself an unsupported inference.

Note 20: Journalism ethics encourages reporters to include the voices of everyday people who are stakeholders to or impacted by the issue in a story. In a story's context, they may NOT be quoted for their formal positions of authority or power (i.e. credentialing). Such Named Person sources will usually not have a formal Title Of Source. (See Source Descriptors for annotating other words that reporters to describe/depict/characterize such sources.)

Note 21 (important): Here is a summary of which source types can carry a Title of Source value: Named Person (yes, when disclosed), Anonymous Source (yes, when disclosed, per Note 17), Unnamed Person (yes, when the reporter uses a credentialing designation instead of a name), Unnamed Group of People (yes, when the group descriptor is credentialing — see Unnamed Group of People definition above), Named Organization (no, per Note 18), Document (no, per Note 18). Title of Source and Source Descriptors are independent fields (see Source Descriptors definition below). A reporter may have included a Title of Source and Source Descriptors, both, or only one of the two, or neither.

Note 21a: A political-orientation or other editorializing qualifier stacked onto a credentialing title is not part of the Title of Source, if removing it still leaves an accurate description of the same formal position. A political-orientation word ("far-right," "progressive," "nationalist") is the reporter's characterization of the source: capture it as a Source Descriptor (see Forming a Source Descriptor). Other qualifiers ("embattled," "controversial") stay out of both fields; they may be Source Justification only if they meet that definition. For example, "the country's far-right interior minister" -> Title of Source "the country's interior minister," Source Descriptors "far-right." This note does not apply to a qualifier that changes which position is described ("interim director" vs. "director") or to an elected official's party designation (see the Title of Source definition); both are part of the title.

Source Descriptors: A Source Descriptor is a reporter's term signaling a category the source belongs to: a role in the story ("candidate," "plaintiff," "critic"), a relationship ("mother," "neighbor"), a social, political or place identity ("Diné," "transgender," "progressive," "Eastside resident"), or an occupation that isn't a credentialing title ("nanny," "truck driver"). For an organization it is the kind of entity; for a document, its genre. A word that describes or rates this particular source ("embattled," "disgruntled," "longtime") signals no category and is not a Source Descriptor. As noted in the other definitions earlier, some sources are characterized by the reporter using one or more short words or phrases that are not formal titles and don't fit the Title of Source definition, but still communicate something meaningful about who the source is or what they're doing in the story. Source Descriptors apply to all the six Types of Sources: Named Person, Unnamed Person, Anonymous Source, Unnamed Group of People, Named Organization, and Document. (i.e. they may be present in the text of the story.)

For human sources (Named Person, Unnamed Person, Anonymous Source, Unnamed Group of People), a word or phrase is a Source Descriptor when it is non-credentialing — it does not denote a formal position of power, authority, responsibility, expertise, or leadership (that's Title of Source, see above), but instead depicts what the source is doing in the story: enacting agency, individually or as a community — including democratic agency, such as voting, running for office, or civic organizing — their relationship to someone else in the story, or an informal social identity. Examples: protestor, activist, advocate, participant, attendee, resident, tenant, candidate, organizer, volunteer, member, paralegal, legal secretary, law clerk, mother, father, sister, brother, friend, neighbor, family member. Source Descriptors is the reporter's characterization of the source. A word the source uses about themselves inside their own quote does not count.

For Named Organization, a Source Descriptor is the organization's category word or short phrase -- what kind of entity it is -- e.g., think tank, nonprofit, advocacy group, watchdog group, coalition, foundation, consulting firm, non-governmental organization, app, utility, state agency. A description of what the organization does or why it exists ("an app that helps renters compare leases") is not a Source Descriptor; it may be Source Justification. If no category word is present in the text, leave Source Descriptors null.

For Document, a Source Descriptor is a word or phrase describing the document's genre or type — e.g., memo, press release, certificate, cease-and-desist notice, report, filing, lawsuit, complaint, ruling, letter, statement, transcript, affidavit, bill, law, policy, database, audit, subpoena, chargesheet, indictment, dataset.

Title of Source and Source Descriptors are independent of each other. A source may have a Title of Source value, a Source Descriptors value, both, or neither — extract whichever the text actually supports. For example, if a Named Person source is introduced as a soil scientist who is also described as a volunteer, extract "soil scientist" (or similar) as Title of Source AND "volunteer" as Source Descriptors for the same source — do not choose one over the other.

Capturing descriptors (along with Titles) supports downstream analysis of how reporters depict sources: some show a source in several facets, others reduce a source to a single label, e.g., "far-left activist." Capture every descriptor the reporter applies to a source, each formed as described below.

Forming a Source Descriptor:

1. Use the reporter's own words for this source, from the article body. Do not infer or paraphrase a label: if the story says a source's home burned down but never calls them a homeowner, "homeowner" is not a Source Descriptor. Likewise, if the reporter never calls a source a "mother" but refers to her only through another person's relationship to her ("she visited her son in the hospital"), do not create "mother" as a Source Descriptor. Generic words are not Source Descriptors either: count nouns ("people," "person," "individuals") and words for the source's role in the reporting itself ("interviewee," "source").
2. Take each descriptor from one phrase, as one unbroken run of the reporter's words. Do not join words from different places in the story, reorder words, or drop a word from the middle of a phrase. You may leave out words at either end, as described in 4.
3. Keep the case and number as written, with two exceptions: lowercase a capital that comes only from the start of a sentence (proper nouns and proper adjectives keep theirs, e.g., "Welsh choir"); and when the reporter names the source as one of a group the reporter labels ("one of the petitioners," "activists like Maria Ortiz"), use the singular ("petitioner," "activist").
4. Keep the head word and the words directly in front of it that sort it into a kind: a noun ("transit advocate," "shipping magnate"), an identity or political word ("Indigenous farmer," "centrist party"), or an adjective that cannot be graded ("mayoral candidate," "former miners," "part-time bus driver"). For a person or group of people, also keep a place the reporter attaches to them ("Eastside resident," "resident of Millbrook," "vendor from Port Alder"). Leave out:
   - words that describe or rate this particular source -- words you could put "very" in front of or say after "is" ("disgruntled," "vocal," "independent"), how long something has been true ("longtime," "lifelong"), and ranks or comparisons ("top," "second-largest," "leading");
   - everything after the head word ("supporter of the ordinance" -> "supporter," "father of three" -> "father," "collective of worried neighbors" -> "collective"), except a place for a person and a group label whose head word means nothing on its own ("people who rent their homes");
   - a named entity attached to the descriptor, such as an organization's name or a named person ("an organizer with the Riverside Tenants Union" -> "organizer," "a friend of Ms. Brandt" -> "friend");
   - for an organization, where it is based ("Denver-based nonprofit" -> "nonprofit");
   - a place or party designation that belongs to an elected official's title (see the Title of Source definition).
5. If something you leave out sits between an identity word and the head word ("a nonbinary 19-year-old cashier"), keep the identity word as its own descriptor ("nonbinary, cashier") rather than joining the two.
6. Separate several descriptors with commas. If none applies, the field is null.

Words left out of Source Descriptors are not lost: they stay in Source Justification when they meet its definition. Do not shorten Source Justification to match Source Descriptors.

Source Descriptors are a story-level value for each source. A descriptor counts wherever the reporter applies it in the article body -- in one of the source's sourced statements or in any other sentence. For example, "Fire officials identified the caller as Ruth Abara, a neighbor of the family" gives Ruth Abara the Source Descriptors "caller, neighbor," even if her only sourced statement comes later. Put the source's descriptors on every row for that source, including rows before the sentence where a descriptor appears, the same way Title of Source is carried forward and backfilled. When the story describes the source in different ways, list the descriptors together; replace one only when the source's status genuinely changes within the story (e.g., "candidate" becoming "councilmember" after an election result reported later). This applies to every type of source: a Named Organization's category word, stated once, applies to all of that organization's rows.

Example 8: Sourced Statement: "Two longtime Harbor Flats residents who belong to the neighborhood tenants union said the new parking rules would push them out." Type of Source: Unnamed Group of People; Name of Source: null; Title of Source: null; Source Descriptors: Harbor Flats residents ("longtime" rates the source and "who belong to the neighborhood tenants union" comes after the head word, so both are left out; the place is kept); Source Justification: Two longtime Harbor Flats residents who belong to the neighborhood tenants union (extracted in full by its own definition). "Harbor Flats residents" appears in both fields. This is intentional.

Example 9: "Dana Whitfield, a vocal critic of the plan and one of the petitioners, said..." gives Source Descriptors "critic, petitioner": "vocal" rates her, "of the plan" comes after the head word, and "one of the petitioners" takes the singular.

Source Descriptors and Source Justification are independent extractions, and they may legitimately overlap in the underlying source text — extracting one does not reduce, replace, or null the other. Source Justification is always extracted in full by applying its own definition (see below), whether or not a Source Descriptor was also found within the same passage. Do not shorten or omit Source Justification text merely because part of it was also captured as a Source Descriptor.

Source Justification

Source Justification refers to additional source characterization, context, explanation that justifies to the reader why the source is in the story or that section of the story, how they are connected to the story. It may be a few words, a part of a sentence, multiple sentences, or a full paragraph. It is extracted only from the actual text in the story, never written or inferred. Source justification may be global - i.e. explain why that source is being included in the story as a whole, and it could have local significance in a particular part of the story or paragraph, where the reporter has added some contextual lines involving the source before quoting the source. That text is source justification, it explains why the source may be saying that or how they came to say that. It may give the source’s statement(s) additional credibility or elevate their significance.

Sometimes the source justification is part of the source's introduction, but it might also come later in the story after some statements have been attributed to the source. For named persons, the source justification text may carry more details about expertise that is not evident from the Title of Source. It may also portray the lived experience of the sources, especially when they are stakeholders to the issue being reported on, who are taking action on an issue, have a participatory role, have witnessed something happen, or are a co-litigant in a lawsuit, etc. It may show the source’s direct knowledge of, involvement in the specific matter being stated, evidence the statement rests on, or the basis from which the source is speaking to that particular claim.

It is this context that demonstrates the source’s significance in the story for readers. For e.g., someone who went through a period of homelessness may be quoted for their lived experience and opinion about solutions. Someone else may have spent four years waiting to get a job or to get their voting rights back because of a prior felony conviction. The reporter may include specific background a lawmaker may have while quoting them sponsoring or debating a bill in the legislature.

A source may have multiple statements (sourced statements) attributed to them in a story, and every such annotation row is eligible to have a source justification. But not every instance of attribution to a source may have a source justification.

Any of the six defined types of sources may have such justifications and explanations present. For Named Organization sources, Source Justification typically holds whatever the reporter says about what the organization does or why it exists (e.g. mission), provided it is (as noted earlier) being included in the story because what the organization does or is its mission is related to it being quoted or attributed to. It will go beyond a bare category label — for example, an organization called Rentwise may be cited for statistics on rental fees. It may be introduced as "Rentwise, an app that helps renters compare leases". That description has the category word "app" - which maps to the Source Descriptors - while the fuller text "an app that helps renters compare leases" maps to Source Justification.

Note that while source justification is NOT the same as Title of the Source, or Source Descriptor, it may include the words annotated into Title of Source and the Source Descriptor.

Note 22: A mere text of attribution is NOT source justification. For e.g. "Jane Doe said by email", "Ram Kumar posted on X", "ABC Company said on its website", etc., are not valid source justifications for the statements being attributed. "Said", "said by email", "posted on Instagram", etc., are simply the reporter's transparency at work: indications of how (pathway) the reporter was able to access the source. On the other hand, in an attribution that says "according to three people who were present at the meeting", the "three people present in the meeting" is an example of valid source justification. This is because their inclusion in the story has come about as a result of that context.

Note 22a: For an Anonymous Source, the reporter's disclosure that the source asked for or was granted anonymity, with any reason given (e.g., "who spoke on condition of anonymity because they were not authorized to discuss the talks"), is Source Justification for every sourced statement from that source, including when it is a blanket disclosure elsewhere in the story (Note 4). Other justification text about the source can sit alongside it.

Note 23: A source may have multiple sourced statements in a story, and depending on the narrative style of the writer, a global (story-level) source justification text may appear early in the story and more specific or proximate or “local” justifications may appear near later statements or vice versa. Again, source justification may not always be represented. Some reporters signal the credibility of the source using the title, and no additional context may be offered.

Example 10: A source may have a broad range of significance that the reporter may indicate in the text. The source may be an eyewitness, present at a meeting, at a protest, part of deliberations for a decision, a member of a jury, part of an organizing group — all of these signal stakeholdership. The descriptive text fragments or sentences referring to this are valid cases for annotation as Source Justification.

Note 23b: Belonging to a group, community, place or identity that the story's issue directly affects is a form of stakeholdership. When the reporter identifies a source by that membership, the phrase is Source Justification even if the reporter adds nothing else, for example "a member of the Kestrel River Nation" in a story about clinic closures on tribal land, or "a group of neighbors who live beside the proposed warehouse" in a story about the warehouse. Membership the story doesn't connect to its issue is not Source Justification on its own. The phrase often overlaps with Source Descriptors: Source Descriptors keeps only the trimmed label ("member," "group"), while Source Justification keeps the reporter's full phrase.
